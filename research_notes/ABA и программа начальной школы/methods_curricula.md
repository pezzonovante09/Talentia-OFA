# Evidence-based instruction of academic skills in ASD and how ABA curricula map onto school curricula

Scope note: ~21 tool calls (searches and fetches). Primary sources were reached for NCAEP/Hume et al. 2021 (PMC full text), the NSP Phase 2 list (National Autism Center page), the VB-MAPP content-validity study (Padilla & Akers 2021, PMC full text) and the VB-MAPP publisher page. Many technique-level questions (errorless learning vs least-to-most, error correction, EBI efficiency) were covered only through abstracts and secondary summaries in search results, and those items are labelled as such. Statements without a source appear under Gaps or Inferences.

## Q1. Which practices are evidence-based for academic skills (NCAEP 2020 and NSP Phase 2), with evidence counts and age 6–11 coverage

### Takeaway
NCAEP (Hume et al. 2021 / Steinbrenner et al. 2020) names 28 focused EBPs from 972 studies (1990–2017). Only 151 studies measured academic outcomes, and the authors call academic outcomes under-studied. The core ABA teaching procedures (prompting, reinforcement, time delay, DTT, task analysis, visual supports, technology-aided instruction, direct instruction) are the ones on the list that are relevant to teaching academic content. NSP Phase 2 (2015) gives 14 "Established" interventions for under-22s at package level ("Behavioral Interventions", "Natural Teaching Strategies", etc.), but I could not retrieve its breakdown by skill and age.

### Cited Findings
- NCAEP third-generation review: 28 focused intervention practices met EBP criteria — [Hume et al. 2021, JADD 51(11):4013–4032 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/)
- The 28 EBPs: Antecedent-based interventions (ABI), AAC, Behavioral momentum intervention (BMI), Cognitive behavioral/instructional strategies (CBIS), Differential reinforcement (DR), Direct instruction (DI), Discrete trial training (DTT), Exercise and movement, Extinction, FBA, FCT, Modeling, Music-mediated intervention, Naturalistic intervention (NI), Parent-implemented intervention, Peer-based instruction and intervention, Prompting (PP), Reinforcement (R), Response interruption/redirection, Self-management, Sensory integration, Social narratives, Social skills training, Task analysis (TA), Technology-aided instruction and intervention (TAII), Time delay (TD), Video modeling (VM), Visual supports (VS) — [Hume et al. 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/)
- Evidence base: 972 acceptable articles in total, i.e. 545 new primary studies from 2012–2017 plus 427 from the 1990–2011 review. Coverage is 1990–2017 — [Hume et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/)
- Number of studies per EBP (1990–2017): Prompting 140; Reinforcement 106; Video modeling 97; Naturalistic intervention 75; Social skills training 74; Visual supports 65; Differential reinforcement 58; Parent-implemented 55; CBIS 50; ABI 49; DTT 38 — [Hume et al. 2021, Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/)
- Studies measuring academic outcomes: 55 in 1990–2011 and 96 in 2012–2017, 151 in total. The authors write that "the number of studies addressing these outcomes remains low relative to the number of studies" — [Hume et al. 2021, Table 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/)
- Ages: most participants were preschool children (43%) or elementary-age children (57%). The EBP-by-outcome-by-age matrix appears only as an image (Fig. 4), so it could not be extracted — [Hume et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/)
- NSP Phase 2 (2015) lists 14 Established interventions for under-22s: Behavioral Interventions; Cognitive Behavioral Intervention Package; Comprehensive Behavioral Treatment for Young Children; Language Training (Production); Modeling; Natural Teaching Strategies; Parent Training; Peer Training Package; Pivotal Response Training; Schedules; Scripting; Self-Management; Social Skills Package; Story-based Intervention — [National Autism Center, Autism Interventions](https://nationalautismcenter.org/autism/autism-interventions/)
- NSP Phase 2 covers studies published from 2007 to February 2012. "Behavioral Interventions" is the only Established intervention for adults 22 and over. The per-intervention detail is published in *Findings and Conclusions: NSP Phase 2* (April 2015) — [National Autism Center, Phase 2 page](https://www.nationalautismcenter.org/national-standards-project/phase-2/)
- Per the ASHA Evidence Map summary, NSP Phase 2 rates Behavioral Interventions as Established for ages 3–21 — [ASHA Evidence Map summary (search result)](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/d5d9d5d7-4c39-4d8d-8bc1-d052b3af7cab)
- Copies of the full NSP2 report circulate, e.g. at [caba.ped.muni.cz](https://caba.ped.muni.cz/media/3349016/findings-and-conclusions2015.pdf) and [narc.uitm.edu.my](https://narc.uitm.edu.my/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/NSP2.pdf). I did not read them.
- A related study of elementary-school EBP use: Sam et al. (2020) on autism EBPs in elementary schools, indexed by WWC — [IES WWC study 90313](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/90313) (not read)

### Inferences
- For an app that teaches school content, the most relevant NCAEP practices are prompting, time delay, reinforcement, DTT, task analysis, visual supports, TAII, modeling/video modeling, direct instruction, and ABI (which includes choice and task interspersal). They have the largest evidence bases, and they are the procedures used in academic-instruction studies. This is inferred from the list and the study counts, not from the age-by-outcome matrix, which I could not read.
- Only about 151 of 972 studies measured academic outcomes, so any practice's evidence specifically for academic targets at ages 6–11 is a small fraction of its total count. Do not quote "140 studies" for prompting as evidence for academic skills.
- NSP Phase 2 works at the package level ("Behavioral Interventions" covers prompting, reinforcement, DTT and similar). It is less useful than NCAEP for choosing specific procedures.

### Gaps
- NCAEP Fig. 4 (EBP × outcome × age 0–2/3–5/6–11/12–14/15–22) was not extracted, so I cannot confirm which EBPs have academic evidence specifically at 6–11. The NCAEP PDF URL tried (ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/.../EBP Report 2020.pdf) returned 404. The matrix is in the full report and on the NCAEP website.
- The NSP Phase 2 counts (the number of studies for under-22s, Emerging/Unestablished counts, skill targets per intervention including "academic", and age 6–9 detail) were not retrieved. The Utah PDF copy redirected to a broken URL.

## Q2. DTT vs NDBI vs incidental teaching for academic skills

### Takeaway
DTT and NI (naturalistic intervention) are both NCAEP EBPs: DTT has 38 studies and NI 75. NDBI was framed by Schreibman et al. (2015) as a merged behavioral and developmental approach for young children. Its evidence comes mostly from toddlers and preschoolers and from social-communication outcomes. Whether DTT or NDBI is better for academic content at ages 6–10 is mainly expert opinion. I found no head-to-head trial on academic outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- NDBI definition: delivered in natural settings, shared control between child and therapist, natural contingencies, several behavioral strategies used to teach developmentally appropriate skills — [Schreibman et al. 2015, JADD 45(8):2411–2428 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25737021/); [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4513196/)
- Schreibman et al. is a review and position paper, not a trial. Its "empirically validated" claim rests on the existing evidence for toddler and young-child interventions — [Schreibman et al. 2015 (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4513196/)
- In NCAEP 2020, DTT has 38 studies and Naturalistic Intervention has 75 (1990–2017) — [Hume et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/)
- NSP Phase 2 lists both "Natural Teaching Strategies" and "Pivotal Response Training" as Established, alongside the "Behavioral Interventions" package — [National Autism Center](https://nationalautismcenter.org/autism/autism-interventions/)

### Inferences
- In practice, DTT fits discrete, well-defined academic targets that need many practice opportunities: letter and number identification, sight words, math facts, matching and sorting, and conditional discriminations in match-to-sample format. These map directly onto tablet task formats such as choose, point and match.
- Naturalistic and incidental teaching fits generalization and the use of academic skills in context (counting real objects, requesting by number, reading labels). It needs a live adult and natural motivation. An app can support it, for example with prompts suggesting what the adult can practise at home, but cannot deliver it. This is inference, not evidence.
- NDBI evidence is concentrated below age 6, so applying NDBI at ages 6–10 for school content is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- I found no comparative study of DTT vs NDBI vs incidental teaching on academic outcomes at ages 5–10.

## Q3. Prompting, error correction, reinforcement, high-p/interspersal, mastery criteria, maintenance and generalization for academic targets

### Takeaway
Prompting (140 studies), time delay and reinforcement (106) are among the best-supported practices. However, the comparative literature (least-to-most vs most-to-least vs time delay, and different error-correction procedures) shows that the most efficient procedure differs from child to child. That argues for assessing per learner rather than choosing one procedure for everyone. High-p sequences have strong meta-analytic evidence for compliance (Tau-U = .87) but only preliminary evidence for acquisition. The common 80%-across-3-sessions mastery criterion may be too low for maintenance.

### Cited Findings
- Prompting (140) and Time delay are both NCAEP EBPs. Reinforcement has 106 studies and Differential reinforcement 58 — [Hume et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/)
- Kodak et al. (2016, JABA) compared five error-correction procedures on sight words and prepositions with children with autism. The assessment found an efficacious and efficient procedure for every participant, but which procedure was best differed by child (one was most efficient for 4 of 5) — [abstract via search: JABA 10.1002/jaba.310 (Zendy)](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/jaba.310); [summary (secondary)](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/aba-fundamentals/examination-of-efficacious--efficient--and-socially-valid-error-correc/)
- Foran-Conn et al. (2021) compared most-to-least prompting, "no-no prompting" and responsive prompt delay in DTT. Responsive prompt delay was at least as effective for 3 participants, and time to mastery varied — [Bangor University record](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/a-comparison-of-most-to-least-prompting-no-no-prompting-and-respo/)
- Moore et al. (2018): some children with autism respond poorly to traditional least-to-most prompting in conditional-discrimination training. They compared error-correction procedures — [Springer, J Behav Educ 10.1007/s10864-018-9305-z](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10864-018-9305-z) (abstract only)
- Chazin & Ledford (2021) compared constant time delay with the system of least prompts on efficiency and child preference — [record (search result)](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4KQJKLN9) (full text not read)
- Barbetta et al. compared immediate vs delayed error correction on sight words with five students with developmental disabilities. The search flagged an inconsistency in the citation year (1993/1994) — [PMC1297789](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1297789) (not read in full)
- High-probability request sequence (2–5 easy, known tasks before a hard one; also called interspersed requests or behavioral momentum): a 2024 meta-analysis covering 40 years of research found Tau-U = .87 for compliance in children with ASD — [Sayar et al. 2024, Behavioral Disorders (SAGE)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01987429231224044)
- Cowan et al. 2017 single-case meta-analysis: support for behavioral momentum (especially the high-p command sequence) for compliance and on-task behavior, not for acquisition — [Springer 10.1007/s10803-017-3076-6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10803-017-3076-6)
- Acquisition evidence for high-p in DTT is preliminary. One single-case study (child aged 3.5) found higher orienting to the instructor (M = 85% vs 36%) and higher accuracy (M = 72% vs 57%, with overlap) — [search result, x-mol record](https://x-mol.com/paper/1761289516958322688) (not verified in full)
- Mastery criteria: a practitioner survey found 80% accuracy across 3 consecutive sessions to be the most common criterion. Richling, Williams & Carr (2019, JABA 52(3):701–717) found this may not produce maintenance for some learners (4 participants with developmental disabilities) — [JABA abstract via British Library](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100087247818_0x000001); [dissertation version](https://core.ac.uk/download/304650182.pdf)
- A secondary commentary reports that in Richling et al., 90% across 3 sessions did not produce consistent maintenance and 100% across 3 sessions worked best. Fuller & Fienup (2018) found 90% within one 20-trial session effective — [commentary, Behav Anal Pract 10.1007/s40617-019-00365-2 (mirror)](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40617-019-00365-2.pdf) (secondary; not verified against the original)
- A later systematic review of mastery criteria reports that higher criteria tend to produce better maintenance — [summary (secondary)](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/systematic-review-of-acquisition-mastery-criteria-and-statistical-anal/)

### Inferences
- Product implication: build the prompt and error-correction procedure as a configurable parameter that a specialist chooses per child, rather than as a fixed rule. The Kodak 2016 and Foran-Conn 2021 results show that the most efficient procedure is learner-specific.
- A mastery criterion of 80%×3 is common but weak for maintenance. A stricter criterion (≥90%, or a set number of independent trials) plus scheduled maintenance probes is defensible. Final thresholds are a specialist decision.
- Interspersing mastered (high-p) items with acquisition items mostly serves engagement and compliance. Its contribution to learning speed is not established.

### Gaps
- Errorless learning (most-to-least prompting, 0-s time delay) vs least-to-most specifically for academic targets: I found no systematic review in this session. Head-to-head single-case studies exist, but the results are mixed and learner-specific.
- Token economies in academic instruction: I retrieved no source specific to ASD and academics. Reinforcement as a whole is the NCAEP EBP (106 studies).
- Multiple exemplar training and programming common stimuli (Stokes & Baer 1977 tradition): I retrieved no source in this session.

## Q4. Stimulus equivalence / EBI and PEAK-E for academic skills

### Takeaway
EBI is theoretically attractive for academic content because training a few relations (spoken word → picture, spoken word → printed word) can produce untrained ones (printed word → picture, reading comprehension). In learners with ASD, however, emergence is inconsistent: in McLay et al. (2013), 5 of 9 studies were positive and 4 variable. The PEAK evidence is developer-led and early-stage.

### Cited Findings
- McLay, Sutherland, Church & Tyler-Merrick (2013), "The formation of equivalence classes in individuals with autism spectrum disorder: a review of the literature", *RASD* 7(2):418–431 — [NTU repository record](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/34538/)
- Later papers report that McLay et al. found 5 of 9 studies positive and 4 variable for emergence of untrained relations, and called for research on when learners with ASD show emergence (secondary report; primary not verified) — [PMC9745008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9745008); [PMC8149511](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149511)
- A 2023 scoping review (Shawler et al.) concluded that the best procedural parameters for forming equivalence classes in individuals with ASD are still unknown — [summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/procedural-parameters-in-equivalence-based-instruction-with-individual/) (secondary)
- PEAK structure: four modules, PEAK-DT (Direct Training), PEAK-G (Generalization), PEAK-E (Equivalence) and PEAK-T (Transformation), with 184 skills each. DT and G target foundational language, academic and social skills. PEAK-E targets reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity and equivalence — [Perspectivas literature review](https://revistaperspectivas.emnuvens.com.br/perspectivas/article/view/750); [ONTABA presentation](https://ontaba.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Promoting-the-Emergence-of-Advanced-Knowledge.pdf)
- Dixon et al. (2017) published a critical review of all peer-reviewed PEAK publications up to April 2017. Most of the reviewed work is by the developer's group — [The Behavior Analyst, 10.1007/s40614-017-0119-4](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40614-017-0119-4.pdf)
- A construct-validity thesis reports preliminary evidence of construct validity and internal consistency of the PEAK pre-assessments — [core.ac.uk thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/326489012.pdf)
- About 14% of ABA professionals (n = 197) report using PEAK, vs about 45% using ABLLS-R (Padilla 2020b, as cited) — [Padilla & Akers 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7808702/)

### Inferences
- EBI is worth considering as an efficiency strategy for app content: train spoken word → picture and spoken word → printed word, then test printed word → picture without training it. Because emergence is unreliable in ASD, the app would need to probe the emergent relations and fall back to direct teaching when they fail to appear.
- PEAK is not yet independently validated, so it should not be the backbone of the curriculum without specialist input.

### Gaps
- I found no independent meta-analysis of EBI or PEAK-E outcomes on school academic content (reading or math) for ages 5–10.
- No efficiency data (trials or time saved) was extracted.

## Q5. VB-MAPP, ABLLS-R, PEAK, AFLS and Essential for Living: academic content, prerequisites, and mapping to school standards

### Takeaway
VB-MAPP covers developmental ages 0–48 months, and its early-academic content (reading, writing, math) sits in Level 3 (30–48 months). ABLLS-R covers 544 skills across 25 areas that typically developing children acquire *before kindergarten*, including Math (R), Writing (S) and Spelling (T). AFLS has a School Skills module, and Essential for Living is a functional curriculum for learners with limited repertoires. State education agencies (Pennsylvania, Kansas) have published VB-MAPP-to-standards crosswalks, but these are alignment charts, not validated equivalences. The VB-MAPP's own content-validity study found weak support for age-appropriateness in Reading, Writing and Math. Treating levels as grades is not supported.

### Cited Findings
- VB-MAPP: 170 milestones in 3 levels (0–18, 18–30, 30–48 months). The publisher states these are developmental age ranges. Skill areas include mand, tact, echoic, intraverbal, listener, motor imitation, independent play, social play, VP-MTS, linguistic structure, **group and classroom skills**, and **early academics**. It also has a Barriers Assessment (24 barriers, e.g. prompt dependency) and a Transition Assessment (18 areas) for decisions about "a less restrictive educational environment" — [Sundberg, VB-MAPP page](https://marksundberg.com/vb-mapp/)
- The publisher notes that some children with ASD are "off the grid" in reading, writing and math while remaining low in social, play and verbal-operant skills. For academic work it points to other resources: Sam Blanco's Common Core ABA curriculum books and *Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons* — [Sundberg, VB-MAPP page](https://marksundberg.com/vb-mapp/)
- Content validity (Padilla & Akers 2021, JADD 51(11):4054–4066): overall content-validity evidence was moderate to strong, but only 53 of 170 milestone items (31%) had CVRs significantly greater than zero. Domain relevance was moderate to strong for 31 of 34 domains, and representation for 23 of 34. Level 3 academic domains were weak: Reading domain-relevance CVI 0.14 with 63% support for age-appropriateness; Writing 46% age-appropriateness support ("limited"); Math CVI 0.26 with 67%. VP-MTS age-appropriateness support fell from 80% at Level 1 to 49% at Level 3. Subject-matter experts said "the age range was too wide to accurately assess skill level" and asked for normative data. The authors recommend supplementing the VB-MAPP with other assessments — [Padilla & Akers 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7808702/)
- ABLLS-R (Partington; original 1998 with Sundberg; revised 2006): 544 skills in 25 areas that "most typically developing children acquire before they enter kindergarten". The academic areas include R = Math (numbers, counting, less/more/equal, basic addition and subtraction), S = Writing (coloring, drawing, copying, writing) and T = Spelling. The Guide covers writing IEP goals — [Wikipedia: ABLLS](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Assessment_of_Basic_Language_and_Learning_Skills); [Different Roads (vendor)](https://difflearn.com/products/revised-ablls-set)
- ABLLS-R validity: in a 2018 study, at least 5 of 6 expert panelists rated 441 of 544 items (81%) "essential" — [Wikipedia: ABLLS (citing a 2018 study)](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Assessment_of_Basic_Language_and_Learning_Skills)
- ABLLS-R is used by about 45% of ABA professionals (Padilla 2020b) and about 40% use it for programming (Austin & Thomas 2017) — [Padilla & Akers 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7808702/)
- AFLS (Partington & Mueller 2012): criterion-referenced assessment, tracking system and curriculum guide with six modules, including Basic Living Skills and **School Skills**. The protocols contain task analyses of skills for participation in family, community and work settings — [Missouri State fact sheet](https://autismtraining.missouristate.edu/factsheets/Autism_Assessment_Overview_Fact_Sheet.pdf); [Different Roads, AFLS School Skills](https://difflearn.com/products/afls_school_skills_assessment_protocol)
- Essential for Living (McGreevy): a functional life-skills curriculum and assessment for children and adults with limited repertoires and moderate-to-severe problem behavior. It centres on the "Essential Eight" skills, with the EARP as its assessment record — [EFL Intro User Guide (SWSC copy)](https://www.swsc.org/cms/lib04/MN01000693/Centricity/Domain/130/Patrick%20McGreevyEFL-Intro-UserGuide.pdf)
- Published crosswalks: Penn State autism outreach (PaTTAN-related) handouts align VB-MAPP milestones to the Pennsylvania Core Standards, including a pre-K version linking early mand milestones to pre-K listening, speaking and reading standards — [Penn State handout 1](https://storage.outreach.psu.edu/autism/46Handout1_0.pdf); [handout 6](https://storage.outreach.psu.edu/autism/46Handout6.pdf); [handout 11](https://storage.outreach.psu.edu/autism/46Handout11.pdf)
- Kansas TASN: VB-MAPP alignment to the KSDE Essential Elements (alternate standards), with grade sections including kindergarten — [TASN ATBS](https://ksdetasn.org/atbs/vb-mapp-alignment-to-the-ksde-essential-elements)
- I found no outcome study linking VB-MAPP scores to kindergarten readiness or school academic outcomes. The crosswalks are alignment documents, not validation studies — search result synthesis ([Padilla & Akers 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7808702/) does not address school readiness)

### Inferences
- The likely chain of prerequisites (typical in practice, not formally validated) runs: VP-MTS (matching identical, then non-identical) → listener responding / receptive identification → tact → LRFFC and intraverbal → early academics (letters, numbers, reading, writing, math in VB-MAPP Level 3 and ABLLS-R Q–T). An app can serve the matching, receptive identification and selection-based academic stages well, because selection responses (point/choose/match) fit a touch screen. Tact and intraverbal require vocal responding and an adult.
- Level 3 of the VB-MAPP corresponds to developmental age 30–48 months, i.e. before grade 0 / kindergarten. Even a learner who has mastered all of Level 3 has only pre-kindergarten-type academics. School grades 0–4 content lies beyond VB-MAPP and mostly beyond ABLLS-R. The Sundberg page itself defers to separate Common Core-aligned ABA curricula for K–1.
- Caution against equating levels with grades: (1) the levels are developmental-age bands, not grades; (2) the academic domains have the weakest content-validity support; (3) profiles in ASD are often uneven (high academics, low verbal or social skills), so one "level" can misrepresent the child; (4) state crosswalks map to alternate or early standards, not to grade-level mastery.

### Gaps
- I found no published Common Core alignment of ABLLS-R, PEAK or AFLS (other than vendor claims) and no validation study of any crosswalk.
- I could not confirm whether ABLLS-R has a separate Reading area. Commonly cited lists include Q = Reading, but the retrieved sources named only R/S/T. Check the protocol.
- I found no Russian or Kyrgyz curriculum crosswalk for any of these instruments; none was searched for.

## Q6. Inclusion and transition to general education: "learning-to-learn" and group-instruction readiness

### Takeaway
VB-MAPP explicitly includes "group and classroom skills" and an 18-area Transition Assessment for decisions about placement in less restrictive settings. Beyond that, readiness criteria for group instruction rest mainly on expert opinion: attending, imitation and observational learning, following group instructions, turn-taking, asking for help, and finishing non-preferred tasks. Practitioner sources note that teams disagree on readiness, and that some data suggest group instruction can be as effective as or more effective than 1:1 for acquisition.

### Cited Findings
- VB-MAPP includes "group and classroom skills" as a skill area. Its Transition Assessment (18 areas) supports IEP decisions about "a less restrictive educational environment" — [Sundberg VB-MAPP page](https://marksundberg.com/vb-mapp/); the Transition Assessment was excluded from the content-validity study — [Padilla & Akers 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7808702/)
- A practitioner article (reported in search results as from a Melmark clinical team) says educational teams often disagree about readiness for group instruction, and that fear of skill loss keeps students on 1:1 longer than needed. It calls for explicit assessment tools and cites Melton et al. (2013) as showing group instruction outperforming 1:1 for acquiring new skills — [Autism Spectrum News](https://autismspectrumnews.org/?p=2348) (expert opinion; attribution and the Melton data are not verified)
- Practitioner readiness lists include turn-taking, requesting help, accepting alternatives and completing non-preferred tasks, with a phased approach that builds these first and then increases inclusion time — [practitioner blog](https://hirasmus.com/2024/09/17/from-circle-time-to-classroom-confidence-shaping-school-readiness-in-aba/) (expert opinion)
- Difficulty learning from group demonstration is often attributed to missing prerequisites such as generalized imitation, which can be taught — [practitioner article](https://autism-skills.pages.dev/articles/can-autistic-children-learn-by-watching) (expert opinion, low-quality source)
- A summary of research on preschool ABA classroom practices (Fleury et al. 2015 and a 2017 systematic review) reports gains in academics and self-care but small gains in social inclusion — reported via search results from a practitioner article ([learning how to be a student overview](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/learning-how-to-be-a-student--an-overview-of-instructional-practices-t/)) (secondary; not verified)
- Penn State autism outreach presentation materials on classroom and learning readiness — [Penn State presentation](https://storage.outreach.psu.edu/autism/75and87Presentation.pdf) (not read)

### Inferences
- A tablet app used one-to-one with an adult cannot teach group-instruction readiness directly. It can strengthen individual learning-to-learn behaviors (attending to a stimulus, waiting for an instruction, responding to an instruction from a distance, working through a short independent task sequence, tolerating new stimuli). Those behaviors are building blocks for group participation. Inference only.
- Gating "academic" content on pre-academic prerequisites (stable matching, receptive identification of the relevant stimuli, responding to an instruction without prompts) is consistent with the structure of VB-MAPP and ABLLS-R. No validated readiness cut-off exists, so the specialist should set the criteria per child.

### Gaps
- I found no validated instrument or empirical cut-off for "ready for group instruction" or "ready for academic targets".
- I did not retrieve primary studies on group instruction vs 1:1 (Melton et al. 2013; Taubman et al.; Ledford et al. reviews of small-group instruction for students with ASD).
- I did not retrieve BACB guidance on academic instruction. The BACB does not appear to publish curriculum guidance; the CASP practice guidelines were not checked.
